In ancient Greek philosophy, as soon as 'soul' came to differentiate animate and inanimate things, plants were included among the soul-havers. Empedocles gave plants souls and referred to them as animals, precisely because they were animate--alive. Later, Plato described plants as having a 'desiring' and 'sensing' soul, which, although the lowest of the various souls, were also endowed with intelligence since, according to Plato, there was no sensation without intelligence.

The origins of dismissing the complexity of the organisms around us seems to have roots in Ancient Greece, starting with Plato making a distinction between the rational man and those that can sense and desire but cannot reason -- namely, women, children, plants, and animals. Aristotle intensified that hierarchy by defining a ladder of life, a _scala naturae_, with plants at the bottom. He went as far as to say that plants not only lacked intelligence, but the capacity to sense at all.

Fast forward till the 20th century where Carl Jung made the pithy observation that modern man does not see God because he does not look low enough. Modern science tends to strip wholeness away in lieu of the small and discrete, which only exacerbates this problem.
